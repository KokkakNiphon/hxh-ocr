Read the Hunter × Hunter alphabet

Point it at Hunter × Hunter script and it reads the kana: every line on the page, across or down. Small int8 models (4.7 MB) run here in your browser, so your picture never leaves your device. As far as we know, it is the first OCR for this alphabet.

        How it works

        The Hunter alphabet is a one-to-one cipher for Japanese kana. Voiced kana add a small dot at the lower right of the base glyph, p-kana a small ring.

        Three small models, all trained only on generated images (no real manga yet), run one after another. A line detector (a DBNet-style segmenter on a MobileNetV4 backbone) looks at the picture shrunk to 640 px (960 px with Accurate) and marks every text line, its slant and whether it runs across or down; the boxes are numbered in reading order (columns right to left). Each line is then cut out straight, turned gray, scaled to 48 px tall and padded to 768 px, and a line reader turns it into kana: one reader for horizontal lines and one for vertical columns, which it sees turned on their side. The readers output 73 hiragana, space, digits, ! ? : / . - and the × of episode titles; katakana and small kana come out as full-size hiragana.

        Fast or Accurate. There are two sets of models. Fast (about 1 MB) is quick and also looks at zoomed-in tiles of a big photo; the live camera always uses it. Accurate (about 3.6 MB, loaded after Fast) has a wider detector and wider readers: it reads photos of screens, cards and signs much better and takes about half a second for a big photo on a laptop. Switch between them under Models, above the picture; the page is read again.

        Everything runs in a lean int8 engine written in Rust and compiled to WebAssembly, so nothing is uploaded.

        Live camera. The camera view is read a few times a second: the detector and the readers run on every processed frame, and each line shows the reading it got most often over the last few frames so the labels don't flicker. Freeze (or tap a line) to keep the picture and see every line's details. Where the browser can't open a camera here, live mode runs on a moving example page instead.

        Tips. On a big photo, small text can be missed by the page pass: tap it, and the detector looks again around your tap at a higher resolution. If a line still isn't found, drag a box around it. About sixteen glyphs fit in one reading; longer lines get squeezed. The small dakuten and handakuten marks are the hardest part: get close enough that they are visible. Uncertain characters are underlined; tap one to see the runner-up.
